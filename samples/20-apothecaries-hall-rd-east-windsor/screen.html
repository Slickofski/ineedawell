The paperwork, step by step

Permits, order of operations, and who does what: the part nobody explains

Well permits for this town run through the North Central District Health Department (NCDHD). Fees below are planning context, not quotes: verify with the district before acting.

  1. Before you commit: get driller quotes (You). Get 2 to 3 quotes from Connecticut-licensed well drillers. Ask each one: what depth would you permit for this lot, and what exactly do I pay if the hole comes up dry?
  2. Verify the driller's license (You). Well drillers must hold a Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection license. Check it before you sign anything: an unlicensed driller can't pull your permit.
  3. File the well permit (Your driller files it; you pay the fee). Your driller signs the state permit application as applicant and files it with the North Central District Health Department (NCDHD). The fee is set by the district, not the state: it is $200 in the North Central District and $150 in Bloomfield's district, to give the local range. Confirm the current fee with the district before the driller files. Nothing gets drilled before this is approved.
  4. Pass the site and setback review (The health district). The district confirms the proposed well spot meets CT setbacks (Public Health Code §19-13-B51d): 75 ft from septic and other pollution sources for a typical home well: the band follows the home's required withdrawal rate (its water demand), not the well's tested yield; the 150 ft band is for larger or shared supplies that must deliver 10 to 50 gpm, and 200 ft above 50 gpm. Plus 25 ft from surface water and drains (50 ft in the higher bands). One rule can change this number: if the soil drains very fast (perc faster than 1 minute per inch) and ledge rock is shallow (leaching bottom less than 8 ft above it), the state doubles the well-to-septic distance: 150 ft for a typical home. That is only known after a perc test, which happens at permit time. This is the step that kills bad lot layouts: better to learn it now than after you own the lot.
  5. Drill the well (Your driller). You pay for the attempt whether the hole comes up wet or dry: that's why the cost band in this snapshot carries the dry-hole contingency. Get the driller's dry-hole policy in writing.
  6. Lab-test the water (You, through a certified lab). Every new CT well must be lab-tested on the state-required baseline panel: 15 tests, including arsenic and uranium (CGS 19a-37(c)(2), since October 1, 2022). If the well serves an existing structure, a first-draw lead sample from the plumbing is also required. Ask the lab whether radon testing makes sense for your lot too: the water section above flags what your area's geology suggests.
  7. Get the health authority's compliance determination (Your local health authority). The lab results go to your local health authority, which determines whether they comply with the state maximum contaminant levels. The well may not be used for domestic purposes until the health authority makes that determination (CGS 19a-37(c)(2)).
  8. Keep your paperwork (You). Get a copy of the well completion report your driller files with the state, and keep it with the deed. Future buyers, and future you, will want it.
