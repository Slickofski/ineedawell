The dry-hole truth

The thing nobody tells you about dry holes.

You pay for the hole even if it’s dry.

Under the standard driller contract, the attempt is billable whether water comes up or not. If the first hole comes up dry or low-yield, the ladder is: drill deeper, hydrofracture the bedrock, drill a second well, or add storage. Each rung costs real money — which is why every snapshot carries a +$10,000–$25,000 contingency on top of the base estimate.

If a quote doesn’t mention this, ask about it before you sign anything.

Good to know

Good to know.

Short lessons from real well work — the headline is the takeaway, and the why is behind the toggle.

Before you drill

Gallons per minute is only half the story — ask how far the water dropped.

A well that makes 10 gpm while its water level barely falls is strong; one that makes 10 gpm by emptying itself is not, even though both filings say "10 gpm."

The details

Most CT wells are tested with compressed air or by bailing, and the driller normally works the well right down to the bottom — so read a filed yield as a good day's result, not a promise, and make any driller you hire talk through what happened to the water level during the test.

A low-yield well can still run a house — the borehole itself is a storage tank.

A standard 6-inch well holds about 1.5 gallons per foot of water column above the pump, so 200 feet of column is roughly 300 gallons in reserve before the aquifer does anything.

The details

A 1 gpm well refilling overnight can bank hundreds of gallons and cover a morning of showers out of storage; the flip side is a high-yield well with a shallow column can run dry in one long draw — almost no buffer. Read a filed yield together with the water column, not alone.

Already have a well

Pump short-cycling? Check the tank before you blame the pump.

A well pump that clicks on and off rapidly is usually a waterlogged pressure tank, not a dying pump.

The details

The tank's air cushion is what gives the pump long, smooth cycles; when the air leaks out, pressure swings instantly and the switch chatters the pump — which is what actually kills pumps.

Quick check: knock on the tank. Top should sound hollow, bottom dull. Dull all the way up = waterlogged; it needs an air charge or a tank.
